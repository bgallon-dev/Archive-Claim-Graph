Reading an answer

What this system does, what the labels mean, and what to check before you trust it

What this system is

Gemynd answers questions from an archive by finding individual claims in the source documents and assembling an answer from them. It is not a search engine and it is not a chatbot with general knowledge — it only knows what has been extracted from this collection.

That has a practical consequence: if the archive doesn't say it, the system can't answer it, and a thin answer usually means thin evidence rather than a failed search. The answer tells you which of those it was — see "When there's nothing to find" below.

What a claim is

A claim is a single factual statement pulled from one sentence of one document, kept with a pointer back to that exact sentence. Everything the system tells you is built from claims, and every claim can be traced to its source.

This is the part worth checking. Click any citation pill in an answer to open the source document, scrolled to the sentence the claim came from and highlighted in the original text — including its original spelling and any OCR errors. If a claim looks wrong, the source will usually tell you why in a few seconds.

Epistemic status: how the source stated it

This label describes the original author's confidence, not the system's. It is taken from how the sentence was written.

observed
The author recorded it as something directly seen, counted, or measured.
estimated
The author gave a figure but signalled it was approximate ("about 3,000").
reported
The author was relaying someone else's account, not their own observation.
uncertain
The author hedged — "possibly", "appeared to be", "may have".

An "uncertain" claim is not a low-quality claim. It is an accurate record of a source that was itself uncertain, and for many research questions that hedging is the finding.

Confidence: how sure the extraction is

Confidence is a different axis, and it is the one most often misread. It measures how reliably the system converted a sentence into a structured claim — not whether the underlying fact is true, and not how important it is.

A low-confidence claim usually means the sentence was hard to parse: damaged OCR, an unusual phrasing, an ambiguous reference. It does not mean the archive is unreliable. Treat low confidence as "check this one against the source", not as "this is probably false".

A high-confidence claim can still be wrong, if the document itself was wrong. The system faithfully records what the archive says, including where the archive is mistaken. Verification is still your job — the citations exist to make it quick.

Claim type

Each claim is sorted into a category defined by this collection's configuration. The categories are how the system narrows a search, so a query using this collection's own vocabulary will generally retrieve more than a paraphrase. If a claim looks mis-categorised, that's worth reporting — it affects what future queries find.

Inferred sentences

Sentences the model wrote to connect the evidence — rather than lifting from a source — are marked as inferred. They carry no citation because nothing in the archive states them directly. They can be useful summary, but they are the model's reasoning, not the record. Weigh them accordingly.

When there's nothing to find

An answer distinguishes between three very different situations, and the difference matters for research:

  • Didn't search — the query didn't trigger that line of retrieval at all.
  • Searched, found nothing — the archive appears not to contain it.
  • Found, ranked lower — evidence exists but didn't make the cut for this answer.

Only the second is evidence of absence, and even then only within this collection. When results were trimmed to fit, the answer says what was dropped — including claims lost to unreadable OCR.

If something looks wrong

Please report it. Wrong answers, missing sources, confusing labels, and slow pages are all useful — during a pilot, a problem you don't report is a problem nobody can fix. Use Report a problem at the bottom of the sidebar; it records the page you were on along with your note.
